A decision is easier to discuss when the assumptions behind it are visible. The argument can then move from whether a proposal feels right to what it depends on.

## Start with the question

A useful question describes a choice someone can make. It names the constraint, the people affected, and the information that would change the decision.

The answer may still be uncertain. Making the uncertainty explicit gives the team something concrete to examine.

{{< quote author="Alex Morgan" source="Example notebook" >}}
Write down what would change your mind before the discussion begins.
{{< /quote >}}

## Make the assumptions visible

Three ordinary questions can expose a surprising amount of hidden disagreement:

1. What do we expect to happen?
2. Which observations support that expectation?
3. What will we do if the expectation is wrong?

These questions do not remove judgement. They give it a structure that other people can inspect.

### Keep a record

A short written record gives a later reader the context that a meeting cannot preserve. It distinguishes what was known from what was assumed.

That record also makes it possible to learn from a result without rewriting the history of the decision.

## Leave room for revision

A decision can be clear without being permanent. The conditions for revisiting it can be as important as the original conclusion.
