What do the data tell us? The data tell us this. The data say that. We looked at the data, and found the other. Variations of this phrase abound in podcasts, TED talks, and  scientific papers. According to Google Scholar, the data tell us about [finance and income inequality](https://www.jstor.org/stable/20111834), [teacher induction](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/003172171209300811), and [the success or failure of primary second/foreign language programmes in Asia](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/14664208.2011.609715). The data seem to tell us quite a bit!

I disagree. I think the data don't tell us anything.

<!--more-->

## The Data Tell Us I'm Right

My disagreement begins with the phrase "the data tell us". This phrase is heavily leaned-on in the social sciences. Freakonomics Radio - an otherwise excellent podcast - is a particularly egregious offender, so I'll pick on one of their shows to use as an example. Discussing [What Will College Look Like in the Fall (and Beyond)?](https://freakonomics.com/podcast/covid-19-college/), the podcast guest[^1] comments,

> The data tell us that the primary impediment is actually not financial. The primary impediment is lack of cultural readiness.

With respect, the data tell you nothing of the sort. This phrasing is designed to convey the illusion of objectivity, leveraging the credibility that Enlightenment principles of objectivity, reason, and the scientific method have earned over centuries. 

Let me reword the quote to make it more accurate, highlighting in bold the key elements:

> Based on **my analysis** of **a particular data set**, **I argue** that the primary impediment is actually not financial. The primary impediment is lack of cultural readiness.

This wording highlights that the speaker has collected a set of data, using collection and recording methods that are open to challenge; has conducted an analysis, which may be flawed; and are making an interpretation of the analysis and a corresponding argument, to which there may be a counterveiling interpretation and argument. It's fair to say that the speaker is actually making a much more opinionated claim than it at first appears. Opinionated rather than subjective, because the latter term leads us inevitably to a descent into postmodernism[^0]; a fate to be avoided at all costs.

## Hidden Defects

Of the above three elements, the collection of the data is the least controversial and most widely discussed. Without delving deeply into issues of bias, particularly in the social sciences, suffice it to say that methods of data collection vary from slightly imperfect to completely invalid. Most pernicious is the tendency to take a data source of doubtful integrity that happens to be there and proceed to load layers of explanatory demand onto it on the basis that it's the best data available, and some data is better than no data.

Less discussed but also relatively uncontroversial is the opinionated nature of the interpretation of analyses and the arguments that result from them. Few would claim that an analysis of a data set admits precisely one, incontrovertable interpretation. Rather more appear to be content to gloss over this possibility when speaking about their research.

Perhaps most controversially, it has been my observation that many, perhaps most, analyses are conducted with no regard whatsoever to the assumptions underlying the methods being used, and their suitability for the task at hand. This is not helped by the fact that many statistical methods have, by comparison to those found in other fields of Applied Mathematics, a poor theoretical foundation relative to the frequency with which they are employed. One need look no further than statistical models that have entered the popular consciousness such as the "Hot Hand" Effect or Fermi's Paradox to see that prominent statisticians disagree vehemently with each other about the fundamental premises of each other's methods. The point to take away from this is not that statistics is wrong or has nothing to say, but rather that there can often be a lot of ambiguity present in the method of analysis itself, and that this ambiguity usually flies under the radar of those employing the method.

## Malice Aforethought

There are a few possible reasons why people might claim that "the data tell us" this or that.

The first is that they may simply have not thought much about it, and it's what everyone else says. The second is that they genuinely believe they're making an objective statement of fact. The third reason is that they are aware that they are making an argument rather than stating a fact, but believe that the ambiguity just leads the discussion in an unproductive direction. The fourth is that they are well aware of the distinction between these ways of speaking, and use it deliberately to shut down debate. 

## A Simple Fix

Regardless of the context, the fix for the false impression created by claiming that "the data tell us" something is to acknowledge the opinionated nature of experimentation and data analysis. 



[^0]: Unfortunately, there exists a particularly ludicrous argument that objective truth doesn't exist, and that "subjective truth" is just as valid. We can therefore abandon science, reason, and the Enlightenment in favour of so-called lived experience. Lest I be tarred with this brush, let me state unequivocally that I will have no truck with this kind of postmodernist nonsense. If you're looking for a rejection of reasoning from data, look elsewhere.
