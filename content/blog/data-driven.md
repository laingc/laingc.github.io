It's hard to find an enterprise these days that doesn't aspire to be more data-driven in its decision-making. The idea of replacing intuition and received wisdom with measurement and rigour is a fine thing, and it's a welcome development to see companies taking that more seriously.

The reader may be surprised to learn that I am a critic of data-driven decision-making; not because the data-driven enterprise removes human judgement (as many detractors would have us believe), but because it leaves it in. 

In this article, I argue that being data-driven is a poor substitute for much more powerful paradigms, namely being model-driven and software-driven. Moreover, I contend that the goal of being data-driven is an enticing trap, preventing the firm's evolution.

### Bad With Numbers

One of the central planks of the data-driven thesis is that humans are quite bad with numbers. Numbers that are very large or very small, things that happen very quickly or very slowly, very small percentages of very large figures. If humans were good at these things, Bill Gates would never have given us Excel.

Data-driven decision making appears to account for this by measuring things more-or-less accurately, then summarising the data for executives to make decisions with. The trouble with this approach is that these summary statistics are numbers, and humans are bad with numbers. As can be attested to by anyone who has ever had to unproductively explain why we should look at the median and not the mean, making decisions with statistics simply transfers the problem of being bad with simple numbers to being bad with complicated numbers. 

### The Data Tell Us

As Ronald Coase famously quipped[^0],

> If you torture the data long enough, it will confess to anything.

In my experience, when I encounter people clearly torturing data to create a narrative they like, they will chuckle appreciatively at this quote, agree with it, then return contentedly to their torturer's implements.
